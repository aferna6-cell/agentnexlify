# A Watershed Year for Chatbot Safety: 14 New State Laws Enacted So Far in 2026

**Transparency Coalition · July 22, 2026**

## The Headline

Fourteen states enacted chatbot safety legislation in the 2026 sessions — more than in all prior years combined. The 2026 sessions marked the point at which AI chatbot regulation moved from a handful of pioneer states to a mainstream legislative activity.

Momentum came from three converging pressures: documented harms to minors from companion chatbots, consumer deception complaints from undisclosed AI in customer service, and the absence of federal action.

## The Four Recurring Requirements

Across the fourteen statutes, four provisions appear most often:

### 1. AI Disclosure

Users must be told they are interacting with an AI, not a human. Nearly universal across the fourteen. Variations:

- Timing: at the outset of the interaction (most common) vs. on user request (weaker) vs. both
- Prominence: "clear and conspicuous" is the recurring standard
- Persistence: some statutes require recurring reminders in long sessions

### 2. Crisis Intervention Protocols

Where a chatbot may encounter a user in crisis, statutes require detection of suicidal ideation or self-harm content and referral to crisis resources such as the 988 Suicide & Crisis Lifeline. Several go further and require the operator to publish or report referral protocols.

### 3. Minor Protections

The category with the sharpest requirements:

- Prohibitions on sexual content in interactions with known minors
- Age-appropriate design duties
- Parental notification provisions in some states
- Recurring reminders that the interlocutor is an AI — commonly at intervals such as every three hours of continued interaction

### 4. Restrictions on Therapeutic Claims

Multiple states now restrict chatbots from presenting themselves as licensed therapists or providing mental-health treatment without human clinical oversight.

## Scope: What Is Covered

The critical question for most businesses is whether their bot falls in scope. The statutes generally target **companion chatbots** — systems designed for sustained, relationship-like, human-mimicking interaction.

Commonly carved out or treated more lightly:

- Customer service bots handling transactional queries
- Booking, scheduling, and support assistants
- Internal enterprise tools not facing consumers
- Bots with narrow, task-bounded scope

Commonly in scope even when not marketed as companions:

- Bots with a named persona and open-ended conversation
- Bots that encourage extended engagement
- Bots reasonably likely to be used by minors

The carve-outs are not uniform. A business operating in many states should assume the broadest applicable definition rather than the narrowest.

## Enforcement Landscape

- State attorneys general are the primary enforcers, generally under unfair-and-deceptive-practices authority
- **Private rights of action** appear in several of the minor-protection statutes — a meaningful escalation in exposure
- Penalties are typically per-violation, which scales with interaction volume
- Some states include cure periods; several do not

## Federal Picture

No comprehensive federal chatbot statute exists. Proposals to preempt state AI regulation have been introduced but not enacted. Absent preemption, the operating assumption should be that the patchwork continues to expand through the 2027 sessions.

Federal agencies have applied existing authority — deception and unfairness enforcement reaches undisclosed AI and false claims about AI capability regardless of state law.

## Compliance Guidance for Operators

1. **Implement disclosure now.** It is the cheapest requirement, the most universal, and the one most likely to be enforced.
2. **Answer honestly when asked.** "Are you a bot?" must return yes. Test this explicitly — many deployed bots deflect.
3. **Add crisis detection and referral** if there is any plausible path to a user in distress reaching the bot.
4. **Assess companion classification** per product surface, in each state where users live.
5. **Build human escalation** and make it visible.
6. **Do not make therapeutic claims** in marketing or in the bot's own responses.
7. **Log compliance artifacts** — disclosure shown, timestamp, and version — so obligations are provable.
8. **Track the 2027 sessions.** More states will act; several 2026 laws have phased effective dates.

## Assessment

The 2026 session established disclosure as the settled national baseline even without a federal statute. The unsettled frontier is companion-AI definitions and affirmative safety-design duties, where state approaches diverge most and where the compliance cost is highest.

For businesses deploying customer-service and booking chatbots, the practical near-term obligation is narrow: disclose, answer honestly, escalate to humans, and avoid therapeutic framing.
