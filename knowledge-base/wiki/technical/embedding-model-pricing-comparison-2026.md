# Embedding Model Pricing Comparison — OpenAI, Cohere, Voyage, Google in 2026

TokenBurner's 2026 embedding-pricing teardown puts a number on a question every RAG team revisits: how much does choosing the right embedding model actually move the all-in bill, and where is over-optimizing a false economy? The headline answer — OpenAI text-embedding-3-small at $0.02/M tokens is the volume-cost floor, but Voyage and Cohere often deliver better retrieval quality per dollar — matches what AgentNexLiFy already runs in production. Our knowledge base uses voyage-3-lite at 512 dimensions exactly because the per-dollar retrieval quality wins on our chunk-size and recall constraints, and because the dimension reduction from 1024+ to 512 cuts pgvector storage and query latency without measurably hurting top-K relevance on our wiki corpus. This article codifies the teardown's findings against our existing [[pgvector-postgres-vector-search]] and [[anthropic-contextual-retrieval]] decisions and identifies where we are and are not following the cost-optimization advice.

The 2026 pricing table is straightforward at the volume level. OpenAI text-embedding-3-small lists at $0.02/M tokens at 1536 dimensions. text-embedding-3-large is $0.13/M at 3072 dimensions — 6.5x cost for higher-quality retrieval. Cohere embed-v3 (English) sits at ~$0.10/M at 1024 dimensions with strong retrieval benchmarks. Voyage voyage-3 is ~$0.06/M at 1024+ dimensions, explicitly tuned for RAG and where TokenBurner places its quality-per-dollar recommendation. Google text-embedding-004 is ~$0.025/M at 768 dimensions, competitive with the OpenAI small tier at slightly higher cost but lower dimensionality. The pricing has compressed considerably since 2024 — every provider now sells under $0.20/M, and the cheap tier has stabilized at $0.02-0.03/M, which is the floor below which the per-token bill becomes irrelevant compared to LLM generation cost.

The decision framework TokenBurner offers is a three-cost-layer view: embeddings (one-time per chunk), vector DB (storage and queries), and LLM (generation per query). Most public-facing pricing posts focus on the LLM line item, but at 1M chunks indexed once at $0.10/M tokens, the embedding bill is $100. If the index re-embeds on every document update, that number scales with update frequency. Embedding spend is therefore "5-15% of total RAG bill" in TokenBurner's framing — non-trivial but rarely the dominant line item. The corollary is the rule the article repeats: do not sacrifice retrieval quality to save pennies on embeddings, because every miss in retrieval forces a more expensive LLM call (or a wrong answer that costs trust). Our [[encore-pgvector-guide-2026]] already makes the same point from the latency angle — vector-DB latency is invisible behind LLM generation time, so optimizing it past a threshold is wasted work.

Practical batching advice in the article is non-negotiable for backfills. Provider async/batch endpoints exist for exactly this case: avoid per-request HTTP overhead, stay under per-minute rate limits, and complete a 1M-chunk backfill in hours rather than days. For one-time backfill workloads where retrieval quality during the backfill window is irrelevant (you index then test), local sentence-transformers models like all-MiniLM-L6-v2 are free at the per-token line item — you pay only the GPU/CPU runtime, which on a backfill of 1M chunks is hours of compute on a $1/hr cloud instance. The architectural pattern then is "embed once with a free local model for backfill, switch to hosted for queries if you need maximum quality" — but few teams take the second step, because the cost delta is small and the operational complexity of running two embedding paths is large.

The deduplication-by-doc-hash advice is the single highest-leverage cost optimization the article highlights. Tracking content hashes per chunk and only re-embedding new or changed content turns a cron that re-embeds the entire corpus every refresh into one that touches only the delta. AgentNexLiFy's KB compile already follows this pattern at the article level — re-runs are idempotent because the `kb_articles` UPSERT keys on slug — but we are not currently hashing chunks, so a content tweak in one section of an article re-embeds the whole article. At our current volume (87 articles, ~2k chunks total), this is invisible; at 10x growth, hash-based chunk tracking becomes the difference between a 30-second compile and a 5-minute one. The pattern fits into the [[supabase-ai-production-checklist]] HNSW + pre-warm framework already in production.

Dimension-vs-cost tradeoff is the part of the article that maps directly to our voyage-3-lite choice. Smaller dimensions (768-1024) are cheaper to store in pgvector and faster to query; the article frames total cost as embed price + storage + query cost, not just embed price. Voyage's 1024-dim default and our 512-dim variant trade some retrieval ceiling for substantially lower storage footprint and query latency. The math is concrete: at 1M chunks, 512 floats per chunk is 2GB raw vs 1024-dim's 4GB. HNSW index overhead is roughly 1.5x the raw, so 3GB vs 6GB. On Supabase, that's the difference between fitting in shared-buffer pages and not. The retrieval ceiling for our wiki corpus is well under what 512 dimensions can capture, so we keep the storage savings and the latency wins without measurable recall degradation in the [[anthropic-contextual-retrieval]]-style pre-chunk-context pipeline we use.

The advice TokenBurner explicitly calls "when not to obsess" is the most useful frame for AgentNexLiFy's current volume. Under 100K chunks, embedding cost is in the tens of dollars — focus on retrieval quality and LLM cost. Stable index with rare updates means embedding is a one-time cost, so picking for quality (Voyage) rather than pennies (OpenAI small) is the right move. Both apply to our wiki: ~2k chunks is two orders of magnitude below the threshold where embedding cost matters at all, and the corpus updates incrementally rather than re-indexing daily. Our voyage-3-lite spend is under $1/month at current volumes — the optimization to make is retrieval quality, not embed price. This is the article's central thesis applied to our specific situation, and it confirms the existing architecture is correctly tuned for our scale.

## Key Concepts

- **Embedding cost layer** — One of three RAG-stack cost layers (embeddings, vector DB, LLM). At realistic volumes embedding is 5-15% of total spend; LLM generation typically dominates. Optimizing embeddings without measuring downstream LLM cost is a false economy.
- **Quality-per-dollar retrieval** — Provider comparison metric that trades embed price against retrieval-benchmark accuracy. Voyage and Cohere typically win this metric for RAG workloads despite higher per-token price than OpenAI's small tier, because better retrieval reduces wrong-answer LLM calls.
- **Doc-hash chunk tracking** — Optimization where each chunk's content hash is stored alongside its embedding. Re-embed only chunks whose hash changes between syncs, eliminating the cost and time of re-embedding unchanged content on every refresh cycle.
- **Local backfill** — Pattern where one-time index population uses sentence-transformers (free, GPU-bound) instead of hosted APIs (per-token billing). Trades operational complexity (two embedding paths) for backfill cost reduction at the 1M+ chunk scale.
- **Dimension vs cost tradeoff** — Smaller embedding dimensions reduce both storage footprint and query latency in pgvector. Total cost is embed-price + storage + query, not just embed-price; lower-dimension models often win the all-in bill even at slightly higher per-token cost.

## Relevance to AgentNexLiFy

Two concrete actions follow. First, our voyage-3-lite at 512 dimensions is the right choice and we should not over-optimize for OpenAI's $0.02/M tier — TokenBurner's quality-per-dollar framing aligns with what we already see in retrieval evals on the wiki corpus, and at ~2k chunks the per-token cost difference is invisible. Second, before scaling past 100K chunks (the threshold the article calls "when to care about cost"), we should add doc-hash chunk tracking to the kb-compile pipeline so that incremental re-embeds only touch changed content. At 10x corpus growth, this shifts compile time from minutes to seconds and keeps embedding cost under the 5-15% band even if voyage raises rates or we move to a higher-quality model for a subset of high-value articles.
