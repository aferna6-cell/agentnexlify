# California's SB 243 Companion Chatbot Law — Operational Mandate Beyond Disclosure, In Effect 2026

California Senate Bill 243 became operative January 1, 2026 and represents the regulatory pivot point for AI conversation products: it is the first US statute to move past disclosure ("you are talking to AI") and into operational duty (you must implement crisis-referral protocols, you must publish your safety procedures, you must give minors break reminders every three hours). The law is narrowly drawn — it targets "companion chatbots" defined by sustained adaptive engagement designed to meet social or emotional needs — but the boundaries are fuzzy enough that any conversational product marketing relational warmth or persistent memory needs to read the exclusions carefully. The Perkins Coie analysis (Soderquist, Taneja, West, Jan 12 2026) highlights the most consequential design choice: SB 243 carries a private right of action with injunctive relief, actual damages, and attorney-fees recovery, which means plaintiffs' bar gets to police the boundary, not just the AG.

The covered class is precise. A "companion chatbot" produces adaptive, human-like responses AND is designed to engage users in ways that meet social or emotional needs across multiple interactions. The statute then explicitly excludes three categories that cover most B2B SaaS chatbots: customer service / business operations / productivity / technical assistance tools, video game chatbots whose responses are scoped to game topics, and stand-alone voice-activated assistants that don't sustain relationships or elicit emotional responses. AgentNexLiFy's widget — which books appointments, captures leads, and answers FAQs scoped to a tenant's business knowledge base — sits comfortably inside the customer service / business operations exclusion, as documented in [[ai-receptionist-platforms-2026]] for the broader category. The risk surface starts only if a feature roadmap drifts toward "relational" framing — a wellness vertical, a healthcare companion, a mental-health-adjacent integration where the bot "checks in" on users.

The operational requirements stack four duties on covered operators. First, clear AI disclosure when a reasonable person would otherwise believe they are talking to a human. Second, a published crisis-content protocol that prevents the bot from producing suicidal-ideation or self-harm content and refers users to crisis services (suicide hotline, crisis text line) when the user signals risk; the protocol details must live on the operator's website, not buried in a privacy policy. Third, a published "may not be suitable for some minors" disclosure. Fourth — the most operationally invasive piece — when the operator knows the user is a minor, the bot must surface AI-disclosure notifications at minimum every three hours during continuing sessions and must implement reasonable measures to prevent generation of sexually explicit visual material or text encouraging such conduct. The "knows the user is a minor" trigger is the load-bearing phrase: actual knowledge, not constructive knowledge. Operators that deliberately avoid age-collection are not automatically safe — willful blindness will eventually be tested in court — but the standard is closer to age-gating than to age-prediction.

Reporting kicks in July 1, 2027. Operators must file annual reports to California's Office of Suicide Prevention covering (1) the number of crisis-referral notifications issued in the prior calendar year, (2) the protocols implemented to detect, remove, and respond to suicidal ideation, and (3) the protocols implemented to prohibit suicide-related responses. The Office is required to publish the reports — meaning operator safety practices become public-facing by force of statute. For a private competitor that fits the covered class, this turns safety operations into a competitive disclosure surface: weak protocols become discoverable, and crisis-referral counts become a benchmark plaintiffs' lawyers can subpoena alongside the public report.

Enforcement is the design feature. The Companion Chatbot Law creates an express private right of action — a statutory user can sue, seek injunctive relief, recover actual damages, and recover reasonable attorneys' fees and costs. This is the structural difference between SB 243 and prior state chatbot disclosure laws (California's earlier B&P 17940-series bot-disclosure law was AG-only enforcement). Plaintiffs' bar now has a direct fee-shift incentive to test the boundary of "companion" status and the adequacy of "reasonable" safety protocols. The Perkins Coie note flags this in passing but it is the load-bearing detail: SB 243 is the first US AI regulation that monetizes enforcement at the user level, and it does so against a backdrop of "increased efforts by plaintiffs' lawyers to pursue claims based on allegedly addictive features." The federal preemption question is also live — the Trump administration's executive order authorizing federal agencies to issue preemption guidance for state AI laws may reshape the landscape — but until preemption guidance materializes, SB 243 binds anyone serving California users in the covered class.

The compliance posture for any operator near the boundary is to document scope analysis now, not later. Perkins Coie's framing is that companies offering conversational tools that "adapt to users over time, personalize interactions, or market themselves as supportive or relational should carefully assess whether their products could fall within the statute's scope." For AgentNexLiFy, the honest answer today is "no, we are customer service / business operations." The honest answer in twelve months — when tenant memory, personalized conversation history, and emotional-tone AI all advance — could be "the line is closer than it was." A short scope memo on file, dated and signed by counsel, is cheap insurance against later having to reconstruct the analysis under litigation discovery. ## Key Concepts

- **Companion chatbot (statutory definition)** — A chatbot producing adaptive, human-like responses AND designed to engage users in ways that meet social or emotional needs across sustained, multi-interaction relationships. The dual prong is what excludes most B2B tools.
- **Customer service / business operations exclusion** — Chatbots used solely for customer service, business operations, productivity, internal research, or technical assistance are explicitly outside SB 243's scope. This is the safe-harbor most B2B SaaS chatbots ride.
- **Private right of action** — A statutory cause of action that lets affected users sue directly without waiting for an AG enforcement action; SB 243's version includes injunctive relief, actual damages, and attorney-fees recovery.
- **Knowledge-of-minor trigger** — The minor-specific safeguards (3-hour break reminders, sexually-explicit content prevention) attach when the operator "knows" the user is a minor — actual rather than constructive knowledge, but willful blindness is a litigation risk.
- **Annual safety-protocol report** — Beginning July 1, 2027, covered operators file annual reports to California's Office of Suicide Prevention; reports become public, turning safety operations into a competitive and litigation-discoverable surface.
- **Preemption watch** — The federal executive order authorizing agencies to issue preemption guidance for state AI laws is the structural threat to SB 243's longevity, but until guidance materializes, state law governs.

## Relevance to AgentNexLiFy

SB 243 does not bind AgentNexLiFy in its current form — the widget is customer service / business operations and rides the explicit exclusion — but the rule is to document the analysis, not assume it. The operational lift is two artifacts: (1) a one-page scope memo signed by counsel stating that the widget falls within the customer service / business operations exclusion of SB 243, with citations to the statutory exclusion language and to the widget's actual functionality (FAQ retrieval, lead capture, appointment booking, no sustained-emotional-relationship features); (2) an internal feature-review gate that flags any future feature pitch as "companion-adjacent" if it crosses lines like persistent emotional memory, wellness-vertical positioning, or relational-framing UX. The gate fires before product, not after legal — same way the schema-discipline rules fire before backend, not at PR review. The crisis-content protocol is also worth implementing as a defense-in-depth measure even outside SB 243's binding scope, because the same disclosure-and-referral pattern surfaces in TCPA-adjacent FTC AI guidance and in the dental/healthcare verticals where the customer base will eventually have HIPAA exposure.

The competitive angle: GoHighLevel, Smith.ai, Goodcall, AnswerForce, and the AI-receptionist platforms all sit in the same exclusion zone and face the same drift risk. The first vendor in the AI-receptionist category to ship a "companion" feature (memory of customer's name across calls, emotional warmth metrics, "your AI assistant remembers you") is the first vendor to test the exclusion boundary in court. AgentNexLiFy's positioning should stay deliberately transactional and business-operations-framed in marketing copy, because the rhetorical positioning shows up as evidence in scope-determination litigation.
