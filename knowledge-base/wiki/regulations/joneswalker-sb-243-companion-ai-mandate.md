# Jones Walker on SB 243 — The $1,000-Per-Violation Floor and Bipartisan Mandate That Travels

Governor Gavin Newsom signed California Senate Bill 243 on October 13, 2025; it became operative January 1, 2026, making California the first state to mandate specific operational safeguards for AI companion chatbots used by minors. Jason Loring's Jones Walker analysis (October 15, 2025) reads the same statute that [[perkinscoie-ca-companion-chatbot-law-now-effect]] covers but emphasizes two structural details that matter more for risk modeling than the operational checklist: the $1,000-per-violation statutory damages floor (or actual losses, whichever is greater, plus attorney fees) and the 33-3 Senate plus 59-1 Assembly vote that telegraphs copycat legislation in other states is a near-certainty rather than a possibility. Operators that fit the covered class face per-message exposure that mirrors TCPA's $500-$1,500 statutory model — the dynamic that drove the SMS compliance industry — and operators that don't fit the covered class today need to track NY, TX, FL, IL bills that will likely arrive within twelve to eighteen months.

The statutory damages structure is the load-bearing detail. SB 243 lets injured parties recover injunctive relief plus the greater of actual damages or $1,000 per violation, plus reasonable attorney fees. That formula is identical in shape to the TCPA penalty regime ($500-$1,500 per non-consented SMS), which built an entire compliance industry around per-message exposure as documented in [[tcpa-sms-compliance-2026]]. The math compounds fast: a single covered chatbot serving 10,000 California users that fails to issue the required minor-disclosure notification on every session generates 10,000 violations × $1,000 = $10M baseline exposure, before counting per-interaction reminders. The "violation" unit is not yet judicially defined, but plaintiffs' lawyers will argue per-message rather than per-user, because that maximizes statutory damages and the fee shift incentivizes aggressive theory. Operators in the covered class need to log every disclosure event and every break-reminder delivery, both for the July 2027 reporting requirement and for litigation defense — same telemetry pattern, two purposes.

The bipartisan vote count is the second signal worth tracking. SB 243 cleared the California Senate 33-3 and the Assembly 59-1 — vote margins that approximate "youth digital safety" as a non-partisan consensus issue. Loring's note flags this as the structural predictor of copycat legislation: when an issue lands above 90% support in a state legislature as polarized as California's, it travels. The states most likely to ship companion-chatbot bills next are New York (which has the NY RAISE Act in motion as documented in [[gunder-2026-ai-laws-update]]), Texas (TRAIGA framework already enacted, suicide-content add-on plausible), Florida (active state AG involvement in chatbot-harm cases), and Illinois (existing BIPA/biometrics enforcement infrastructure ports easily to chatbot-disclosure regimes). The 14-month runway between SB 243's signing and the July 2027 first-report deadline is the buffer the next 5-10 states will use to draft, debate, and pass parallel statutes — so a covered operator's compliance work in California is investment, not waste, because the same protocol stack will likely be required in 5+ jurisdictions by 2028.

Jones Walker's framing on the operational requirements emphasizes "evidence-based methods for measuring suicidal ideation" — phrasing the statute uses but Perkins Coie's read does not foreground. The phrase matters because it shifts the standard from procedural ("did you implement a protocol") to substantive ("did your protocol use evidence-based detection"). Operators cannot bolt on a keyword filter and call it compliant; the protocol needs grounding in published clinical detection methods (Columbia Suicide Severity Rating Scale, PHQ-9-style screening, validated NLP classifiers trained on annotated suicidal-ideation datasets). This raises the floor on safety-engineering investment for covered operators — a $50K consultancy retainer to build and validate the detection protocol becomes the cheap path, and ad-hoc internal builds become the expensive litigation surface. For the covered class, this is structurally similar to PCI-DSS's "evidence-based controls" framing: you can't self-attest, you need a defensible audit trail.

The California Department of Public Health is the reporting destination Jones Walker cites — a different agency than Perkins Coie's reference to the Office of Suicide Prevention. The statute itself names the Office of Suicide Prevention (a unit within the Department of Public Health), so both readings are technically correct; the difference is emphasis. Jones Walker's framing makes clear the report becomes a public health document, not just a private operator-to-state transmission. Reports are published, meaning the universe of covered operators effectively builds a benchmark dataset of safety-protocol effectiveness that plaintiffs, regulators, and competitors all read. Operators with weak crisis-referral counts relative to their user base become discoverable targets; operators with strong protocols become competitive winners on the "regulatory-grade safety" axis.

The compliance posture for the covered class is therefore three artifacts plus a telemetry stack. First, a published safety-protocol document on the operator's website meeting the statute's content requirements. Second, an evidence-based suicidal-ideation detection layer built on validated clinical methods, not keyword filters. Third, a minor-disclosure-and-break-reminder UX layer that fires on every session start and at three-hour intervals during continuing sessions, with the AI-disclosure notification and break-reminder events logged per-user-per-session. The telemetry is the load-bearing piece — both for the July 2027 annual report and for litigation defense, because in a per-violation statutory damages regime the operator's logs are the only evidence between "we complied 99% of the time" and "we complied 0% of the time, and 10,000 users get $1,000 each."

## Key Concepts

- **Statutory damages floor** — SB 243's $1,000-per-violation minimum (or actual losses, whichever greater) plus attorney fees; the structural feature that turns plaintiffs' bar into the primary enforcement vector and mirrors TCPA's $500-$1,500 per-message model.
- **Per-violation unit (undefined)** — Whether "violation" means per-user, per-session, per-message, or per-undisclosed-minor-interaction is unsettled. Plaintiffs will argue per-message; operators will argue per-user. The judicial answer determines whether 10,000-user exposure is $10M or $10B.
- **Evidence-based detection methods** — Statutory phrasing requiring suicidal-ideation detection grounded in published clinical instruments (Columbia C-SSRS, PHQ-9-style screening, validated NLP classifiers) rather than keyword filters; raises the safety-engineering floor for covered operators.
- **Office of Suicide Prevention** — California's reporting destination for annual SB 243 safety-protocol reports starting July 1, 2027; sits within the California Department of Public Health.
- **Bipartisan-vote-as-copycat-signal** — When a state legislature passes AI safety legislation with 33-3 / 59-1 margins, the issue is non-partisan consensus and copycat bills in NY, TX, FL, IL within 12-18 months are the base-rate expectation, not the tail risk.
- **Per-violation logging discipline** — In statutory-damages regimes, operator-side logs of disclosure-and-protocol events become the dispositive evidence in litigation; absent logs, plaintiffs' theory of universal non-compliance becomes the default.
