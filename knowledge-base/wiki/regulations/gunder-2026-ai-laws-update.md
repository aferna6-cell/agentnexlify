# 2026 AI Laws Update — Federal EO, State AI Acts, EU AI Act, and Consumer Chatbot Statutes

Gunderson Dettmer's February 5, 2026 client alert is the most comprehensive vendor-side summary of the 2026 AI regulatory landscape and supersedes the narrower picture captured in [[us-chatbot-legislation-2026]]. The single most consequential takeaway: Trump's December 11, 2025 Executive Order on "Ensuring a National Policy Framework for AI" signals federal preemption intent, but does not preempt, suspend, or invalidate any current state AI law. Every 2026 state deadline still binds. The compliance reality on the ground is that companies must continue meeting Colorado, California, Texas, and EU obligations during the multi-year period the EO's federal infrastructure is litigated and built out. For AgentNexLiFy, the most operationally consequential single statute is California SB 243, the AI Companion Chatbot Safety Act, effective January 1, 2026, because it carries a private right of action with $1,000-per-violation statutory minimum damages — and because our chat widget is exactly the surface area the law regulates.

The federal EO directs three preemption mechanisms but creates no immediate compliance obligations. First, the Department of Justice and AG must establish an AI Litigation Task Force within 30 days to identify and challenge state AI laws deemed unconstitutional or burdensome to federal policy. Second, within 90 days the Secretary of Commerce, Special Advisor for AI and Crypto, and other White House advisors must publish an "Evaluation" identifying state laws that conflict with the federal "minimally burdensome" framework, and the FCC must begin a rulemaking on whether to adopt a federal AI reporting standard. Third, federal funding eligibility — including BEAD broadband infrastructure grants — can be conditioned on states not enforcing AI laws that conflict with EO objectives. The EO explicitly cites Colorado's AI Act as a candidate for the Litigation Task Force review. The practical guidance from the Gunder team is unambiguous: continue compliance with all existing state AI laws because the EO does not impact those obligations and is likely to face substantial legal challenges. State AGs retain enforcement authority under generic consumer-protection and unfair-and-deceptive-acts statutes regardless of EO outcome, so even if a specific AI statute is invalidated, the underlying enforcement risk persists.

State comprehensive AI governance laws are the heaviest compliance lift but apply primarily above thresholds that exempt SMBs and most early-stage SaaS. Colorado SB 24-205 (Colorado AI Act) takes effect June 30, 2026 (delayed from Feb 1) and applies to "developers" or "deployers" of "high-risk AI systems" used to make "consequential decisions" affecting employment, education, finance, healthcare, or housing. It imposes a duty of care to prevent algorithmic discrimination, requires impact assessments, public website notice, consumer opt-out rights, and consumer appeal mechanisms — all enforceable by the Colorado AG with up to $20,000 per violation. Texas HB 149 (TRAIGA) is narrower, takes effect January 1, 2026, and primarily addresses governmental use plus "clear and conspicuous" notice when consumers interact with AI in regulated contexts. New York S6953-B (RAISE Act) effective January 1, 2027 targets only frontier-model developers with $500M+ annual revenue or NY operations of frontier models — thresholds that exclude AgentNexLiFy and the vast majority of SaaS. California SB 53 applies similarly only to covered frontier developers training in California.

The consumer-facing chatbot laws are where AgentNexLiFy's compliance work concentrates. California SB 243 is the most consequential because it creates a private right of action: any person who suffers an injury from violation may bring a civil action with statutory minimum damages of $1,000 per violation, plus attorneys' fees and costs. SB 243 targets "companion chatbots" — AI systems providing adaptive humanlike responses capable of maintaining ongoing relationship-style interactions. It mandates conspicuous AI disclosure at the start of each interaction, reminders every 3 hours, suitability warnings for minors, self-harm and suicide detection protocols with referral to crisis providers, content restrictions for minors, and (beginning July 1, 2027) crisis-incident recordkeeping and reporting. The California AG also has consumer-protection enforcement authority on top of the private cause of action. The widget chat we ship for tenants is unambiguously in scope when the tenant operates in California, and the engagement-style framing of the law captures even task-oriented assistants whose conversations span multiple sessions.

New York S-3008C, Part U (effective November 5, 2025) parallels California's structure for "AI companions" with a $15,000-per-day penalty for notification and safety-protocol violations, but no private right of action. NY S-3008C, Part X (effective November 10, 2025) covers algorithmic pricing — entities using personalized algorithmic pricing must include the exact disclosure "THIS PRICE WAS SET BY AN ALGORITHM USING YOUR PERSONAL DATA" alongside the price. Use of protected-class data in pricing is restricted. The NY AG enforces both. Maine LD 1727 (effective September 23, 2025) applies generally — using an AI chatbot in trade or commerce in Maine without clear and conspicuous disclosure that the consumer is not engaging with a human is a violation of Maine's Unfair Trade Practices Act, enforced by the Maine AG. Utah SB 226 (effective May 7, 2025) requires that suppliers disclose AI use in regulated-occupation services where the interaction is "high-risk" (sensitive personal data collection or recommendations consumers may rely on for significant decisions), with up to $5,000 per violation.

The EU AI Act is the highest-stakes international regime. Most high-risk AI obligations come into force August 2, 2026 — including documentation, human oversight, post-market monitoring, conformity assessment, and CE marking for systems used in employment, credit, education, and access to essential services. Fines reach €35M or 7% of global turnover for prohibited practices, €15M or 3% for high-risk AI obligations, and €7.5M or 1% for other violations including misleading information to authorities. AI used in employment (recruitment, selection, hiring, promotion, performance monitoring) is automatically high-risk under Annex III. GDPR Article 22 already restricts solely automated decisions producing legal or similarly significant effects, with €20M or 4% turnover fines under the GDPR. The EU Data Act (effective September 12, 2025) governs connected-product data with access-by-design, fair-and-non-discriminatory data sharing, and cloud-switching obligations. Together, the three EU regulations expose even US-only companies whose products are accessed by EU residents.

The practical-guidance section closes with the most important compliance posture for SMB-facing SaaS: even when applicability thresholds exclude the company directly, AI-specific addenda and contractual representations from enterprise customers cascade compliance expectations downstream. Agencies and SMB tenants who themselves serve regulated industries will increasingly demand vendor warranties on training data, audit cooperation, exit and interoperability terms, and allocation of regulatory responsibilities. Companies should exercise caution making guarantees beyond their visibility or control. This is the part of the regulatory environment that touches AgentNexLiFy most directly through tenant contracts in healthcare ([[hipaa-ai-chatbot-compliance-2026]], [[hipaa-compliant-chatbot-vendors-2026]]) and consumer-protection-sensitive verticals (auto, real estate, financial services), independent of whether we cross statutory thresholds ourselves.

## Key Concepts

- **Private right of action** — Statute provision allowing private individuals (not just regulators) to sue for violations. California SB 243 includes one with $1,000-per-violation statutory minimum damages plus attorneys' fees, raising the actual exposure from regulator enforcement risk to mass-action litigation risk. SB 243 is the only 2026 AI law with this structure.
- **Companion chatbot (CA SB 243)** — AI system providing adaptive humanlike responses capable of maintaining ongoing, relationship-style interactions. Operationally, an AI chat widget that retains context across sessions or builds personalized rapport is in scope, even if the primary use case is task-oriented like booking or qualification.
- **High-risk AI system (Colorado AI Act, EU AI Act)** — Categorization for AI systems used in "consequential decisions" (employment, education, finance, healthcare, housing under Colorado) or Annex III contexts (EU AI Act). Triggers risk assessment, documentation, human oversight, and post-market monitoring obligations.
- **Frontier model developer (CA SB 53, NY RAISE Act)** — Developer training models above large compute thresholds (CA) or with $500M+ annual revenue or NY frontier-model operations (NY). Both statutes apply only at this tier — not to most SaaS — but cascade into vendor contract terms downstream.
- **Federal preemption mechanism (Trump EO Dec 2025)** — Three-step process: AI Litigation Task Force challenges state laws, FCC rulemaking on federal AI reporting standard, federal funding conditioned on state non-enforcement. None preempts existing state laws today; all create future risk to states' AI authority.
- **Algorithmic pricing disclosure (NY S-3008C Part X)** — Exact-text disclosure requirement: "THIS PRICE WAS SET BY AN ALGORITHM USING YOUR PERSONAL DATA." Protected-class data usage restricted. $1,000 per violation enforced by NY AG.

## Relevance to AgentNexLiFy

Three concrete compliance actions follow, ranked by urgency. First, California SB 243 is enforceable now and creates direct private-action exposure on our chat widget. We need conspicuous AI-disclosure at conversation start, reminders every 3 hours in long sessions, and self-harm detection with crisis-resource fallback — particularly because our widget is purposely engagement-style and tenants in mental-health-adjacent verticals (counseling, wellness, addiction services) push us into the highest-risk SB 243 surface. Second, NY S-3008C Part U requires AI-disclosure at start of every interaction and reminders every 3 hours; the implementation overlaps with SB 243 and should ship as one feature. Maine LD 1727 and Utah SB 226 are satisfied by the same disclosure pattern. Third, contract addenda from tenants will cascade compliance even where statutes exempt us directly — we should pre-draft an AI-vendor data-processing addendum that allocates training-data provenance responsibility to us, audit cooperation, and explicit exclusion of guarantees on tenant-uploaded content, before enterprise tenants demand bespoke language. The federal EO is monitor-only — keep complying with state laws as if it does not exist, because legally it does not change anything actionable.
