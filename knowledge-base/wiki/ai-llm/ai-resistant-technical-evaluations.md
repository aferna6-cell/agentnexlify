# Designing AI-Resistant Technical Evaluations — Anthropic's Performance Engineering Take-Home

Tristan Hume's January 2026 writeup of Anthropic's performance-engineering take-home is the first public case study of iterating a technical interview against a moving AI capability frontier. Between early 2024 and late 2025, over 1,000 candidates completed the original take-home (a parallel tree traversal optimized against a simulated accelerator), and dozens were hired — including engineers who brought up Anthropic's Trainium cluster. Then Claude Opus 4 beat the test under its 4-hour time limit. Hume redesigned it for version 2; Claude Opus 4.5 beat that too, matching the best human 2-hour performance. The piece documents what worked, what didn't, and why the new version is a constrained-instruction-set puzzle rather than a realistic optimization problem. The lesson generalizes: AI-resistant evaluations now require problems genuinely out of the model's training distribution, not just harder versions of the same problem.

The original design goals were conventional — representative of real work, high signal across a wide scoring distribution, no specific domain knowledge, and fun. The format advantages of a take-home (longer time horizon, realistic no-observer environment, room for tooling) were chosen to distinguish from live interviews where "any knowledge Claude has, the candidate can replicate in seconds." The simulated accelerator included features that make real accelerator optimization interesting: manually managed scratchpad memory, VLIW instruction packing, SIMD vector operations, and multicore distribution. Candidates started with a serial implementation and progressively exploited the machine's parallelism. The test explicitly permitted AI assistance, reasoning that longer-horizon problems are harder for AI to solve completely, so candidates could use AI tools while still demonstrating their own skills. This assumption — that long-horizon real work was inherently AI-resistant — is what Opus 4 and 4.5 disproved.

By May 2025, Claude 3.7 Sonnet had crept up to where over 50% of candidates would have been better off delegating to Claude Code entirely. Pre-release Opus 4 came up with a more optimized solution than almost all humans within the 4-hour limit. The v2 fix was straightforward: use Claude Opus 4 to identify where it started struggling on the original problem, and make that the new starting point. Cleaner starter code, new machine features for more depth, multicore removed (Claude had already solved it and it only slowed development loops without adding signal). Time limit cut from 4h to 2h for scheduling throughput. This worked for several months — until Opus 4.5.

The Opus 4.5 defeat is the most diagnostic moment. Hume watched Claude Code work on the problem for two hours, gradually improving. It solved initial bottlenecks, implemented common micro-optimizations, and met the passing threshold in under an hour. Then it stopped, convinced it had hit a memory bandwidth limit — the same conclusion most humans reach. When Hume told Claude the achievable cycle count, it thought for a while, found the non-obvious trick that exploits problem structure, and implemented it. By the 2-hour mark its score matched the best human performance within the time limit — and that human had made heavy use of Claude 4 with steering. Internal test-time compute harness runs confirmed Opus 4.5 could both beat humans in 2 hours and continue climbing with more time. The dominant candidate strategy would become delegating to Claude Code.

Two failed redesigns illustrate what doesn't work. Attempt 1 — a harder optimization problem (efficient 2D TPU register transposition avoiding bank conflicts) — failed because "engineers across many platforms have struggled with data transposition and bank conflicts, so Claude has substantial training data to draw on." Training-data density is the invisible axis. Attempt 2 — Zachtronics-inspired constrained-instruction-set puzzles with tiny register counts and branch-flag state encoding — succeeded. The puzzles deliberately provide no visualization or debugging tools; building them is part of what's tested. Opus 4.5 failed the medium-hard puzzle in this format. Colleagues less steeped in the problem than Hume still outperformed Claude. The deliberate design choice: "realism may be a luxury we no longer have. The original worked because it resembled real work. The replacement works because it simulates novel work."

The released benchmark numbers quantify the capability gap. On the version-2 instruction set: Claude Opus 4 achieved 2164 cycles after many hours in test-time compute; Opus 4.5 in a casual Claude Code session hit 1790 (matching best 2-hour human); Opus 4.5 in the test-time compute harness reached 1579 in 2 hours, 1487 after 11.5 hours, and 1363 in an improved harness after many more hours. Sonnet 4.5 after "many more than 2 hours" of test-time compute: 1548. Humans with unlimited time still outperform — the fastest submission "substantially exceeds what Claude has achieved even with extensive test-time compute" — but the pool of humans who can beat Claude's best score in a bounded window is small and shrinking. Anthropic released the original take-home publicly as an open challenge: optimize below 1487 cycles and they want to hear from you.

The pattern that generalizes for AgentNexLiFy and other companies hiring engineers: long-time-horizon real-work problems are no longer AI-resistant by default, because models now work for hours with test-time compute. Novel instruction sets, constrained state, and problems requiring debugging tool construction from first principles remain resistant — for now — because they live outside the training distribution. Interview design becomes a moving target where every 6–12 months requires a rewrite. [[claude-opus-4-6-capabilities]] and the Opus 4.7 release notes ([[claude-opus-4-7-release]]) indicate the trend continues.

## Key Concepts

- **AI-resistance (of an evaluation)** — The degree to which a problem requires capabilities that cannot be substituted by delegating to a current frontier model. Measured empirically by running the frontier model on the problem and comparing its output to human candidates under the same constraints.
- **Training-data density** — How much related material exists in a model's training corpus for a given problem type. High-density problems (data transposition, bank conflicts, common ML optimizations) favor models because they have a larger toolbox to draw on; low-density problems favor humans who can reason from first principles.
- **Test-time compute harness** — Runtime infrastructure that gives a model extended deliberation time, repeated iteration, and tool access across many hours. Anthropic's internal harness produced the 1363-cycle best result; represents the upper bound of model capability on a given problem.
- **Sprint-wise negotiation** — Hume's pattern of having generator and evaluator agents negotiate a sprint contract before code is written, bridging high-level spec and testable implementation. Not specific to this article but shapes how the harness patterns from [[effective-harnesses-long-running-agents]] evolve.
- **Novel work vs real work** — The design tension Hume calls out: a test that resembles the actual job (real work) is easier for a well-trained model to solve because the job itself is in-distribution; a test that simulates unusual work (novel work) remains harder for AI but sacrifices candidate-perceived realism.

## Relevance to AgentNexLiFy

Two concrete implications. First, our hiring pipeline (when we eventually hire engineers) should assume AI assistance is default-on for technical assessments. Real-work take-homes — "build this endpoint, optimize this query" — no longer discriminate between strong candidates and weak ones because Claude Code closes most of the gap. We should steal Anthropic's Zachtronics pattern: a constrained-instruction-set puzzle or a problem requiring debugging-tool construction where the AI's training corpus is thin. Second, the same capability arc that breaks take-homes enables our own product roadmap. If Opus 4.5 in a 2-hour test-time-compute harness can match the best human performance engineer, then background agent workflows — nightly commit review, issue-to-PR loop, KB autopopulate — should be increasingly Opus-heavy with explicit test-time-compute budgets (see `.claude/rules/task-budgets.md`). The harness pattern from [[effective-harnesses-long-running-agents]] and the GAN-inspired generator/evaluator architecture in related research are directly applicable to our managed-agents infrastructure.
